A gene essentiality predictor that is accurate across cancer and healthy cell lines must have learnt why each line depends on each gene. Every accurate prediction therefore implies a molecular determinant (e.g., a mutation, copy-number or methylation event, or a mutational or expression signature) that can be read out as a candidate biomarker. Using the essentiality predictor, structure cancer cell lines into gene-dependent subtypes explainable by molecular biomarkers, such that groups of cell lines share a gene dependency for the same molecular reason. Look for subtypes both within and across cancer types, since determinants that recur across lineages are the stronger candidates for clinical translation. Benchmark the recovered biomarkers against standard single-feature association tests, where possible complement with combinatorial CRISPR screens, and control for known confounders (e.g., culture conditions, passenger alterations, genomic co-location, and multiple testing). Validate against established clinical cancer biomarkers and their companion trials, asking whether the subtypes recover known predictive biomarkers with the correct direction of effect, clinically actionable subtypes, and the correct patient population. The biomarker–drug pairs that reached trials provide a ground truth that cell-line data alone cannot supply. Finally, characterise the limits of the approach, identifying which gene dependencies cannot be reliably used as cancer biomarkers and why.
